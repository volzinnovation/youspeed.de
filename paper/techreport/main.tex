\begin{abstract}
This paper evaluates runtime data architectures for low-latency, offline-capable smartphone speed-limit inference using country-scale OpenStreetMap (OSM) data. We identify four data processing scenarios that are compared to find an optimal query and update scenario along three typical query conditions. Hence, we identify the preferred operating point for practical deployment for on-device speed-limit inference as required for Intelligent speed assistance (ISA) applications that are mandatory for new vehicles in the EU since 2022 and open the way for a smartphone and open data based retrofitting approach for vehicles that are already in traffic. Thereby we can strongly support the EU traffic safety vision of reduced crash severity.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Open Data \and OpenStreetMap \and map matching \and mobile GIS \and offline-first \and spatial indexing \and file-based preprocessing}

\section{Introduction}
Excessive speed remains a major factor in crash severity, making reliable speed-limit assistance a core road-safety problem. For intelligent speed assistance (ISA), this creates a practical requirement for map context retrieval that remains accurate and low-latency under storage, memory, and connectivity constraints.

European regulation has made ISA deployment operationally mandatory. Article 6 of Regulation (EU) 2019/2144 requires ISA in new motor vehicles of categories M and N \cite{eu2019_2144}, and Delegated Regulation (EU) 2021/1958 specifies detailed ISA performance and test rules \cite{eu2021_1958}. The rollout is phased: the requirement applies to new vehicle types from July 2022 and to all newly registered cars, vans, trucks, and buses from July 2024 \cite{eu_mandatory_safety_2024}. The European Commission estimates that the broader General Safety Regulation package can prevent approximately 25{,}000 deaths and at least 140{,}000 serious injuries in the EU by 2038 \cite{eu_mandatory_safety_2024}.

A large legacy-vehicle gap remains. Eurostat reports that EU passenger-car stock exceeded 260 million vehicles in 2024 \cite{eurostat_passenger_cars_2026}, while ACEA reports 10.6 million new EU car registrations in 2024 \cite{acea_registrations_2024}. Using registration year as a coarse proxy, even an optimistic post-2024 assumption still leaves roughly 249 million pre-2024 vehicles (about 96\% of the fleet) as potential retrofit targets.

This gap motivates a broad deployment approach based on smartphone sensing and open geospatial data. Eurostat reports mobile-phone internet use of 89\% in cities and 82\% in rural areas in 2024 \cite{eurostat_mobile_internet_2024}, and OpenStreetMap (OSM) offers large-scale, continuously updated road-network attributes relevant for speed-limit inference \cite{osmwiki_highways,osmwiki_maxspeed}.

Against this background, this study examines runtime data architecture as the primary systems bottleneck. We compare four scenarios built from the same national OSM extract: global country-scale indexes (S1), tiled file packs (S2), a single-file spatially indexed database (S3), and a spatially indexed database with additional tile-window prefiltering (S4). The objective is to quantify latency and update-operability trade-offs under both cloud-like and on-device conditions.

\section{Related Work}
Map matching has been studied extensively for GPS trajectories under measurement noise and sampling sparsity. A foundational survey by \citet{quddus2007} systematized geometric, topological, and probabilistic matching strategies and outlined scaling challenges that remain relevant for mobile deployment.

Probabilistic sequence models became dominant for noisy and sparse data. \citet{newson2009} introduced a Hidden Markov Model (HMM) formulation that combines emission and transition probabilities, while \citet{lou2009} proposed ST-matching to jointly model spatial and temporal consistency for low-sampling trajectories. Real-time variants for streaming probe data were then explored in online HMM frameworks \citep{goh2012,he2013}.

Subsequent work strengthened route-level inference in sparse regimes. \citet{rahmani2013} formulated path inference from sparse floating-car data on urban networks, and the Path Inference Filter by \citet{hunter2014} provided a model-based low-latency approach for probe vehicles. Across these HMM/sequence families, the common effect is to keep trajectories on the current road unless turn transitions are sufficiently plausible under topology and motion evidence \citep{newson2009,hunter2014,goh2012,he2013}. Hybrid designs combining shortest-path priors with trajectory evidence further improved low-frequency matching \citep{quddus2015}, and route-choice-aware HMM approaches improved robustness for noisy sparse traces \citep{jagadeesh2017}. Enhanced HMM models that explicitly incorporate topology and traffic constraints also reported improved accuracy and consistency on floating-car data \citep{che2018}.

This structure is mirrored in production engines. OSRM, Valhalla (Meili), and Mapbox map matching expose candidate emission controls, transition plausibility settings (including tunable GPS uncertainty or search radii), and confidence-style outputs for downstream consumers \citep{osrm_match_service,valhalla_meili,mapbox_map_matching_api}. To reduce online transition-check cost, precomputed transition structures have been proposed: FMM uses a UBODT-style table that turns many route-distance transition checks into fast lookups during inference \citep{yang2018fmm}.

These studies primarily optimize candidate selection and path inference given an available road graph. In contrast, our contribution focuses on the systems layer around map data itself: physically tiled, content-addressed assets with independent update delivery and bounded local IO at query time. This targets a complementary bottleneck that is critical for country-scale, offline-first mobile speed-limit inference.

The implemented app runtime is deliberately simpler than full route-level HMM inference. It uses per-fix geometric scoring plus lightweight continuity heuristics (preferred-way hysteresis, same-ref segment promotion, and tunnel gating tied to recent route context) rather than a multi-step probabilistic sequence model. The related-work discussion is therefore used here as architectural context and benchmark motivation, not as a claim that the implementation reproduces the full model class used by prior map-matching systems.

Spatial access methods are central to this systems layer. The R-tree introduced by \citet{guttman1984} established the foundational dynamic index for multi-dimensional range search and remains a standard basis for geographic candidate retrieval in database systems. Here, R-tree-based indexing is evaluated not as a new algorithmic contribution, but as an architectural instrument to control candidate fan-out and improve practical query latency at country scale.

\section{Technical Vision}
\subsection{Purpose}
YouSpeed exists to make intelligent speed assistance practical for vehicles already on the road, not only for newly manufactured cars. The product target is an offline-first smartphone assistant that infers local speed limits with low latency, high availability, and transparent data provenance. The project target is broader: to show, with reproducible evidence, that open geospatial data combined with robust runtime architecture can provide safety-relevant functionality at consumer scale.

This work is intentionally dual-track. One track is scientific and publication-oriented, where architecture decisions are benchmarked and documented under controlled conditions. The second track is product-oriented, where validated architecture choices are integrated into an iPhone app under real update, reliability, and operational constraints.

\subsection{Strategic phases}
The system evolution is explicitly phased:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Open-data bootstrap:} initialize the platform from OpenStreetMap and rule-aware defaults for country-scale baseline coverage.
\item \textbf{Product stabilization:} prioritize fast offline inference, deterministic updates, and measurable reliability on consumer devices.
\item \textbf{Community enrichment:} add user-observed speed-sign signals once install base and observation density are sufficient.
\end{enumerate}
Crowdsourcing is defined as an augmentation layer, not a replacement for OSM baseline data.

\subsection{Crowdsourced speed intelligence}
With sufficient adoption, two user-facing observation channels are introduced while driving. The first is on-device computer vision, where the smartphone camera detects speed-relevant signs when road-forward view is available. The second is voice input with speech recognition for hands-free reporting of sign changes, temporary restrictions, or map inconsistencies. Both channels generate candidate observations rather than immediate shared truth.

Each observation first updates a local device layer with confidence and decay semantics. Observations are then queued for deferred synchronization and promotion only after cross-device corroboration and validation.

\subsection{Trust model without user accounts}
YouSpeed is designed to operate without account authentication. Identity is pseudonymous and device-based. This lowers user friction and supports privacy goals, but shifts quality assurance into architecture: per-device source scoring, temporal/spatial consistency checks, minimum independent confirmations, and explicit conflict resolution.

A single-device report must not overwrite shared truth. Promotion to global data requires corroboration and consistency with geometry and legal plausibility constraints.

\subsection{Layered data ownership and feedback loops}
The data model is layered rather than monolithic:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{External baseline layer:} imported from OSM and related official/open sources.
\item \textbf{Internal global cache layer:} confirmed community observations maintained by YouSpeed.
\item \textbf{Local device layer:} unconfirmed and recently confirmed observations relevant to one user.
\item \textbf{Publication layer:} quality-gated candidates eligible for upstream contribution.
\end{itemize}

Confirmed findings follow two outbound paths: immediate use in the internal global cache and optional moderated feedback to OpenStreetMap via controlled export.

\subsection{Synchronization as a core systems challenge}
The central systems challenge is synchronization across truth sources with different latency and trust levels: local observations, globally confirmed internal data, external map updates, and periodic app bundles. Required capabilities include deterministic merge rules, versioned records, conflict precedence, rollback-safe activation, bandwidth-aware delta sync, offline accumulation, delayed upload, and eventual convergence without destructive overwrite.

\subsection{App-side integration challenges}
Computer vision and speech recognition are treated as separate subsystems with independent quality gates, failure modes, and runtime budgets. Integration happens through a shared observation contract so both channels emit comparable events for local storage, synchronization, and trust evaluation.

\subsection{Regional sharding for large countries}
To control runtime artifact size at country scale, bundle generation follows a threshold rule: if a country PBF exceeds \SI{1}{GB}, the pipeline switches from a monolithic country bundle to regional Geofabrik extracts (for example German Bundeslaender). Each regional v3 bundle carries explicit coverage metadata consisting of a fast bounding box and a region polygon (\texttt{.poly}) artifact.

On device, multiple downloaded regional bundles can coexist. Runtime bundle routing is location-driven: the app first applies bbox prefiltering and then performs polygon containment for the current GPS fix to select the best local DB. This keeps inference fully offline while avoiding mandatory download of a full-country database when regional subsets are sufficient.

\subsection{North-star outcome}
The intended end state is a trusted open-data ISA retrofit platform that remains offline-capable and low-latency while improving continuously through external baselines and validated community observations. In that state, day-to-day user utility, auditable data operations, and reproducible research claims are aligned.

\subsection{Implemented runtime inference pipeline}
To avoid concept drift between architecture text and implementation, this subsection documents the implemented per-fix inference order in the iPhone runtime:
\[
\text{for fix } t,\quad C_t = \{\text{retrieved candidate ways for fix } t\}
\]
\[
s_{\mathrm{geom}}(t,i)=
\begin{cases}
d_{t,i} + \lambda_h \cdot \Delta\theta_{t,i}, & \text{if } v_t \ge \SI{8}{km/h} \text{ and } a_t < 45^\circ \\
d_{t,i}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]
where \(i \in C_t\), \(d_{t,i}\) is the exact geometry distance from fix \(t\) to candidate way \(i\), \(\Delta\theta_{t,i}\) is the bidirectional heading mismatch in degrees, \(v_t\) is vehicle speed, \(a_t\) is heading accuracy, and \(\lambda_h=\SI{1.8}{m/deg}\).

For candidate-trace ranking and diagnostics, the runtime derives a second score that applies continuity class before geometry:
\[
\beta(c)=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{preferredWay}\\
1 & \text{sameRef}\\
2 & \text{linkedWay}\\
3 & \text{recentWay}\\
4 & \text{none}
\end{cases}
\qquad
W_t = 1 + \max_{k \in C_t} s_{\mathrm{geom}}(t,k)
\]
\[
s_{\mathrm{trace}}(t,i) = \beta(c_{t,i})\cdot W_t + s_{\mathrm{geom}}(t,i)
\]
Here \(c_{t,i}\) is the continuity class assigned to candidate \(i\) at fix \(t\). Because \(s_{\mathrm{geom}}(t,i) < W_t\) for every \(i \in C_t\), a one-band change in \(\beta(\cdot)\) always dominates any geometric-score difference within the same fix. The resulting order is therefore lexicographic by continuity class first and raw geometry second: every \texttt{preferredWay} candidate ranks ahead of every \texttt{sameRef} candidate, every \texttt{sameRef} candidate ranks ahead of every \texttt{linkedWay} candidate, and so on; within the same continuity band, lower geometric score still wins.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Candidate retrieval (RTree prefilter).} For each GPS fix, the app queries \texttt{ways\_rtree} in a bounded radius and loads way attributes from \texttt{ways}; if available, \texttt{way\_geom} polyline points are used for exact geometry distance.
\item \textbf{Geometric and directional scoring.} The raw per-candidate score used by the matcher is \(s_{\mathrm{geom}}\): distance plus heading mismatch penalty. Heading contributes only when vehicle speed is at least \SI{8}{km/h} and heading accuracy is better than \(45^\circ\); mismatch is weighted by \(\lambda_h=\SI{1.8}{m/deg}\).
\item \textbf{Continuity context and hysteresis.} The matcher keeps a short continuity context consisting of the previous matched way, recent matched ways, recent street refs, recent tunnel candidates, and a recent GPS-signal-loss flag. If a previous matched way remains plausible, the runtime may keep it within a small slack (\SI{12}{m}) and an accuracy-adjusted distance bound, reducing oscillation between nearby candidates.
\item \textbf{Same-ref transition handling.} If the current best candidate shares the same normalized \texttt{ref} as the previous way, the matcher may promote a segment transition instead of holding the old segment. This transition is preferred once the old segment is near an endpoint and the alternative segment is not materially farther away. The goal is to stabilize progression along one numbered route while still allowing segment changes at intersections.
\item \textbf{Connected-transition gate.} If the bundle contains \texttt{way\_links}, the runtime derives a directed adjacency graph \(E \subseteq W \times W\) from exact shared endpoints. After a warm start of three matched fixes and in the absence of recent GPS loss, candidate selection is restricted to
\[
\widetilde{C}_t=\left\{i \in C_t \mid i=p_t \;\lor\; i \in R_t \;\lor\; \exists j \in R_t:(j,i)\in E \right\},
\]
where \(p_t\) is the preferred way and \(R_t\) is the recent matched-way set. This gate is applied before heuristic ranking and before mini-HMM expansion, which makes disconnected hops impossible once continuity has stabilized while still leaving the first few fixes unconstrained.
\item \textbf{Continuity-aware candidate trace scoring.} For logs, inspector output, and candidate-trace visualization, the runtime computes \(s_{\mathrm{trace}}\) from continuity band plus \(s_{\mathrm{geom}}\). This makes the reported candidate order reflect the effective continuity preference: \texttt{preferredWay} \(<\) \texttt{sameRef} \(<\) \texttt{linkedWay} \(<\) \texttt{recentWay} \(<\) \texttt{none}. As a result, a geometrically good but non-continuous alternative can still rank below a continuity-preserving candidate, while a non-continuous candidate that is not the best geometric option will rank behind both better geometric peers in the same \texttt{none} band and all candidates in stronger continuity bands.
\item \textbf{Speed-source derivation on the selected way.} Speed precedence on the matched way is: explicit \texttt{maxspeed} parse, then inherited regulatory tags (\texttt{maxspeed:type}/\texttt{source:maxspeed}: urban/rural/motorway), then highway-class prior.
\item \textbf{Inside-city classification (primary rule).} If the matched way class is one of \texttt{residential}, \texttt{service}, \texttt{crossing}, or \texttt{living\_street}, the fix is classified as in-city directly.
\item \textbf{Inside-city classification (fallback rule).} For other way classes, the runtime evaluates residential polygon containment in \texttt{areas.residential}. This polygon path is also used by the benchmarked polycontainment query.
\item \textbf{City label resolution for UI and logging.} City name/source metadata is resolved from area/place context (\texttt{areas}, \texttt{city\_boundary}, \texttt{city\_ring}) and attached to the result for traceability; this label path is not the primary in-city classifier.
\item \textbf{Effective speed output and fallback.} At app level, final speed precedence is local per-way override first, then selected-way speed. If selected speed comes only from coarse highway class and in-city status is known, legal urban/rural default (\SI{50}{km/h}/\SI{100}{km/h}) overrides the class prior. If no way speed exists but a way match and in-city status are available, legal default is applied. If only in-city status is available without a way speed, conservative \SI{50}{km/h} fallback is used.
\item \textbf{Tunnel context gating.} Tunnel candidates are filtered before final selection. A tunnel-tagged way is accepted only if it is the previous matched way, if it is connected through shared route identity (\texttt{ref}) and available topology links to recent continuity context, or after a recent GPS-signal loss when the tunnel candidate was already observed in the nearby-candidate set. This reduces false switches to unrelated tunnel-tagged geometry, but it also means that bundles without \texttt{way\_links} topology cannot validate shared-ref portal entry from geometry alone.
\item \textbf{Tunnel context output.} If the selected way passes tunnel gating and carries truthy \texttt{tunnel}, the fix is marked tunnel segment. The UI switches to tunnel icon emphasis, keeps the matched speed limit visible, and suppresses penalty notices while tunnel mode is active.
\item \textbf{Context output for downstream logic.} Each lookup also returns \texttt{way\_id}, street display name (\texttt{street\_name}/\texttt{ref}), city label/source, candidate counts, timing, and tunnel flag.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Tunnel mode state machine}
Tunnel handling in the app is deterministic and not purely tag-driven:
\begin{itemize}
\item Session state is \texttt{inactive} or \texttt{active}.
\item On each fix, \texttt{isTunnelSegment} from the gated matched-way decision drives the state (\texttt{active} if true, else \texttt{inactive}).
\item Entry into tunnel mode is therefore possible only through continuity to the previous route context, through shared-ref portal validation backed by optional \texttt{way\_links} topology, or after a recent signal-loss event that still has a remembered tunnel candidate.
\item If lookup service is unavailable, continuity context records a recent GPS-signal-loss event and tunnel state is reset until the next successful gated match.
\item There is no dedicated portal detector, no inertial tunnel tracking, and no explicit exit hysteresis beyond the next successful non-tunnel match.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Runtime bundle structures used by inference}
The implemented inference stack depends on attributes materialized in scenario bundles (S1--S4), including:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Way-level context in \texttt{ways}:} \texttt{street\_name}, \texttt{ref}, \texttt{approx\_heading\_deg}, \texttt{service}, \texttt{tunnel}, \texttt{highway}, plus speed-related tags.
\item \textbf{Area context in \texttt{areas}:} \texttt{boundary}, \texttt{admin\_level}, \texttt{residential}, \texttt{parking}, and geometry/bbox data for containment and city-label resolution.
\item \textbf{Coverage metadata in manifests:} \texttt{coverage.bbox} and optional \texttt{coverage.poly} artifacts for regional bundle routing on device.
\item \textbf{Bundle update artifacts:} region-scoped manifests, optional split DB parts, rolling delta-index manifests, and compressed SQL patches for incremental bundle refresh on device.
\end{itemize}

\section{International Fine Rules and Country Bundles}
\subsection{Motivation}
The architecture treats speed-limit inference and sanction inference as two coupled but separable concerns. Speed-limit inference is map-driven and query-latency critical. Fine/warning inference is regulation-driven and jurisdiction-specific. For cross-border operation, country-specific legal rules must be versioned and deployed with the corresponding map bundle, otherwise the app can combine correct speed-limit lookup with incorrect warning semantics.

This extension targets the 10 countries with the highest maxspeed availability (Netherlands, Romania, Luxembourg, Belgium, Sweden, Liechtenstein, Iceland, Germany, Monaco, United Kingdom), while keeping the same runtime interface for future country additions.

\subsection{Country-bundle approach}
Generated v3 manifests carry region-scoped artifact names and explicit region identity:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{region}: active bundle region identifier (for example \texttt{germany}, \texttt{karlsruhe-regbez}).
\item \texttt{country\_code}: ISO alpha-3 identifier for the legal regime of the bundle.
\item \texttt{db}/\texttt{db\_parts}: main runtime database artifact(s), named \texttt{<region>\_speeds.sqlite} (or split \texttt{.partNNN} assets).
\item \texttt{self}: manifest artifact, named \texttt{<region>\_manifest.json}.
\end{itemize}

For large countries that are region-sharded, all regional manifests share the same \texttt{country\_code}. Rule selection is resolved at runtime from this code; the app loads bundled \texttt{<ISO3>-rules.json} and falls back to DEU rules if country-specific rules are unavailable. The manifest field \texttt{penalty\_rules} is supported by the app parser as an optional override artifact, and generated manifests use bundled rule files by default.

This makes legal-rule deployment deterministic, cacheable, and auditable in the same way as map artifacts, while preserving offline operation.

\subsection{Operational semantics and limitations}
The app uses the rules for \emph{driver warning and context} only. It does not claim legal finality. Rule files are treated as versioned indicative policy tables that require periodic legal verification, especially where sanctions depend on offender history, discretionary court decisions, or local procedural details not observable in map data.

From a systems perspective, this design keeps the query/update trade-off unchanged for geometric inference while adding a low-cost legal-context layer that scales linearly with country coverage.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Speed regulations in Germany}
For passenger-car inference in Germany, this study uses the legal baseline defined in \S3 StVO: \(50\) km/h in built-up areas and \(100\) km/h outside built-up areas unless traffic signs specify otherwise \cite{stvo_3}. At runtime, final speed precedence is: local user override (if present), explicit way-level \texttt{maxspeed}, inherited regulatory tags, highway-class prior, and finally legal urban/rural fallback. Built-up-area inference follows the implemented app order: highway-class precedence (\texttt{residential}/\texttt{service}/\texttt{crossing}/\texttt{living\_street} \(\Rightarrow\) in-city), then residential polygon containment for all other classes. DE built-up-area sign markers (\texttt{traffic\_sign=DE:310/311}) are retained as optional metadata but are not part of the primary classifier due sparse coverage.

To frame practical impact, Germany also applies escalating sanctions for speeding. Public guidance aligned with the current fine catalog reports, for example, \(+21\) to \(+25\) km/h in urban areas as EUR 115 plus one point in the driving register, and from \(+31\) km/h as EUR 260 plus one point and a one-month driving ban \cite{adac_bussgeld_2025}. These enforcement consequences motivate conservative fallback design in ISA research.


\subsection{OpenStreetMap dataset potential for speed-limit inference}
Tables~\ref{tab:highway_distribution} and~\ref{tab:dataset_profile} summarize the Germany OSM input used throughout this study. The dataset contains 7.96 million car-drivable ways and broad coverage of context objects (administrative boundaries, area contexts, and city place tags), which is sufficient to support both direct speed-limit lookup and fallback context inference.

The data also shows strong promise for practical speed-limit inference. More than 2.63 million drivable ways carry explicit \texttt{maxspeed} tagging, while high-volume road classes such as residential, secondary, and tertiary roads contribute substantial additional network coverage for rule-based inference when explicit values are absent. This combination of explicit speed tags and structured road semantics is a key enabler for scalable, offline-capable ISA research on open data.

Beyond classic speed tags, current runtime bundles carry the tunnel and urban-context features required by the implemented app logic: way-level \texttt{tunnel} attributes for tunnel-mode signaling, and area context (\texttt{residential}, \texttt{boundary}, \texttt{admin\_level}, \texttt{parking}) for city/rural fallback and city labeling.

\subsection{Data processing scenarios (S1--S4)}

\subsubsection{Shared preprocessing and scope}
All four scenarios are generated from the same OSM extract (Geofabrik \texttt{germany-latest.osm.pbf}) \cite{geofabrik2026}. Preprocessing follows a file-based parsing workflow \cite{pyosmium} and does not rely on a separately deployed database server. The retained road network includes car-drivable \texttt{highway=*} categories (major roads, local roads, and connector links), ensuring that scenario differences arise from runtime data architecture and query execution rather than from differing road subsets.
For current bundle generation, retained way geometries are serialized with endpoint-preserving downsampling and a conservative cap of 24 points per way. This cap was selected after inspecting raw node-count distributions on the same retained drivable-way subset for both the Karlsruhe seed PBF and the Netherlands release PBF: the empirical \(p95\) was 16 and 17 nodes, only 1.80\% and 1.94\% of retained ways exceeded 24 nodes, and the cap preserved 95.6\% and 95.9\% of raw polyline points, respectively. A lower knee exists near 16 points, but 24 was chosen as the cross-region default to reduce long-tail truncation in production bundles.


\subsection{Three query types}
We use typical location-based queries to lookup speed limits for a vehicle in motion. For the scenario benchmark, all queries use the same geometric score:
\[
\text{score} = d + \lambda_h \cdot \Delta\theta + p_u
\]
where \(d\) is the geometry term, \(\lambda_h\) heading weight (\si{m/deg}), \(\Delta\theta\) bidirectional heading mismatch, and \(p_u\) optional unknown-highway penalty.
This benchmark score intentionally excludes the runtime continuity-band term \(s_{\mathrm{trace}}\): the benchmark compares geometry-query architectures, whereas continuity-aware ordering is an app-level matching and trace-interpretation layer applied after candidate retrieval.

\textbf{bbox query.} \(d\) is haversine distance from the probe to the clamped point on a candidate bbox. It is the fastest approximation and is used as the coarse filter in every scenario.

\textbf{polyline query.} \(d\) is minimum point-to-segment distance across the full candidate geometry. This gives the highest geometric fidelity, especially on curved and elongated ways.

\textbf{hybrid query.} Candidates are first ranked with bbox distance. A limited top-\(N\) subset is then refined with polyline distance, combining near-polyline quality with lower compute cost.

\item  S1 is the global-index baseline: country-scale index files are queried directly without prior spatial partitioning, which makes it simple but increases broad candidate scans and IO pressure for each lookup.

\item  S3 uses a file-based embedded database with spatial indexing for candidate retrieval. S3 performs direct spatial-window search and then geometric ranking. 

\item S4 also uses a file-based embedded database but adds a tile-membership prefilter before spatial-index intersection to reduce candidate fan-out in dense urban regions.



\end{itemize}
Because S2--S4 runtime artifacts are file-based and immutable per build, they support horizontal cloud scaling through replication across stateless workers and object storage distribution, while also remaining suitable for autonomous offline execution on mobile devices.

\subsection{Measurement scenarios}
We evaluate two deployment modes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Online (cloud-like) scenario:} host-side execution on a desktop-class environment, representing server-style continuous evaluation for online queries (measured without networking overhead).
\item \textbf{Offline (on-device) scenario:} physical smartphone execution with country-scale runtime data stored in the mobile sandbox, representing autonomous offline operation.
\end{itemize}

Both modes are assessed the same way across the four runtime scenarios and the same three query types.

\subsection{Field-log reassessment: tunnels, Autobahn corridors, and model selection}
Replaying the nine bundled inspector logs yields 11{,}259 matched fixes. This corpus includes motorway entry and exit behavior, tunnel approaches, and the corrected Loffenau sequence. The disconnected hop from L 564 onto service way 16654539 stays eliminated after warmup because the shared-endpoint graph gate removes candidates that are not connected to the recent path.

Two matcher profiles were evaluated on the same S3 query layer. The first is a \emph{connected baseline}: nearest-distance selection with connected-way continuity, low-speed heading-change transitions, and tunnel/motorway lock modes that only admit entry or exit at shared nodes. Its bundle stores \texttt{way\_links\_mode=shared\_endpoint\_minimal} with 433{,}504 directed adjacency pairs and no corridor tables, for a file size of 113{,}795{,}072 bytes. The second is the selected \emph{shared-node corridor HMM}: a detailed shared-endpoint graph with \texttt{way\_links\_mode=shared\_endpoint\_detailed}, 435{,}372 directed adjacency edges, 2{,}345 corridor chains, 11{,}961 \texttt{corridor\_progress} rows, and 1{,}656 explicit \texttt{corridor\_pairs}, for a file size of 134{,}434{,}816 bytes.

The corridor precompute uses only shared nodes and branch nodes as legal entry, exit, and split points. Let \(G_k=(V_k,E_k)\) denote the subgraph induced by ways of corridor kind \(k \in \{\text{tunnel},\text{motorway}\}\). Let \(P_k \subseteq V_k\) be the set of nodes shared with admissible outside ways: surface ways for tunnels and \texttt{motorway\_link} ways for motorways. The boundary-node set is
\[
B_k = P_k \cup \{v \in V_k \mid \deg_{G_k}(v) \neq 2\}.
\]
A corridor \(c\) is then a maximal chain of ways in \(G_k\) between two nodes in \(B_k\). For each side node \(p \in B_k\), the bundle attaches a paired approach chain: a local surface chain for tunnels or a \texttt{motorway\_link} chain for motorways, both starting at the same shared node \(p\). This representation captures the ``multiple nodes at entry and exit'' semantics without treating an entire motorway network or an entire same-ref surface network as one corridor.

The selected runtime augments candidate identity \(i \in C_t\) with both corridor identity \(c_t\) and corridor phase \(z_t\), where
\[
Z=\{\text{surface},\text{tunnelEntry},\text{tunnelInside},\text{tunnelExit},\text{motorwayEntry},\text{motorwayInside},\text{motorwayExit}\}.
\]
The mini-HMM recurrence is
\[
J_t(i,c,z)= s_{\mathrm{geom}}(t,i) + \phi_t(i,c,z) + \min_{(j,c',z')} \left[J_{t-1}(j,c',z') + \tau\big((j,c',z'),(i,c,z)\big)\right].
\]
Here \(\phi_t(i,c,z)\) is low only when way \(i\) belongs to corridor \(c\) or to one of its paired approach chains compatible with phase \(z\). The transition term \(\tau\) forbids or heavily penalizes moves that violate shared-node legality, such as \(\text{surface}\rightarrow\text{tunnelInside}\) without a paired portal chain or \(\text{motorwayInside}\rightarrow\text{surface}\) without a paired exit ramp.

Model selection uses a common score that balances replay accuracy, query time, and bundle size. Let \(a\) be hindsight accuracy, \(r\) changed-example recall, and \(u\) unchanged-example accuracy. The accuracy composite is
\[
A = 0.5a + 0.25r + 0.25u.
\]
Let \(t\) be service-query \(p95\) latency and \(b\) bundle bytes, with \(t_{\min}\) and \(b_{\min}\) the best values across compared models. The final selection score is
\[
S = 100 \left(0.7A + 0.2\frac{t_{\min}}{t} + 0.1\frac{b_{\min}}{b}\right).
\]

Under this protocol, the connected baseline yields \(a=93.37\%\), \(r=49.45\%\), \(u=96.67\%\), \(A=0.8321\), geom-log agreement \(80.45\%\), service-query \(p95=0.151\) ms, and \(S=88.25\). The shared-node corridor HMM yields \(a=96.13\%\), \(r=72.51\%\), \(u=97.90\%\), \(A=0.9066\), geom-log agreement \(85.80\%\), service-query \(p95=0.156\) ms, and \(S=91.21\). The corridor model is therefore selected despite an \(18.1\%\) larger bundle and a modest latency increase, because its replay accuracy and stability are materially better.

The tunnel-specific evidence also separates the two models clearly. On the two geom logs, the connected baseline enters tunnels 82 times although the recorded app selections contain 0 tunnel fixes there, whereas the corridor HMM records 0 tunnel selections and avoids those false commits. Across the nine inspector logs, the selected corridor model records 103 tunnel fixes, all concentrated in \texttt{drive\_match\_log-3}; both profiles keep way 313127285 confined to that log and select it 0 times in the other driving logs.

The remaining gap is under-commitment in the geom tunnel logs: the shared-node corridor HMM still does not recover those real tunnel traversals, even though it models legal entry and exit correctly. The field data indicate that tunnel traversal often retains GPS fixes while horizontal accuracy and signal bars deteriorate only later inside the tunnel. The next model improvement therefore belongs in the emission side of the corridor HMM: along-chain portal progress and delayed signal-quality deterioration inside the committed tunnel corridor.

This indicates that tile partitioning improves refresh locality over global-index invalidation, and that S3 remains the stronger update/query compromise among database-centered variants.

To translate these architectural differences into deployment-relevant transfer times, payload transmission is represented as \(T=(B_{\text{payload}}+B_{\text{overhead}})/R\), with \(B_{\text{overhead}}\) modeled as a separate 10\% transport overhead term. For Europe, a practical throughput range is \SI{104.3}{Mbps} to \SI{217.6}{Mbps} for 5G downloads (spectrum-dependent) \cite{opensignal_europe_5g_2024}, with an EU-27 mobile average of \SI{124}{Mbps} in 2025 \cite{eu_connectivity_impact_2026}. Using measured median tile-pack size (\SI{47199}{B}) and measured daily update volumes, indicative transfer times are: (i) 100 median tiles (\SI{4.72}{MB} payload): \SIrange{0.19}{0.40}{s}; (ii) 1000 median tiles (\SI{47.2}{MB}): \SIrange{1.91}{3.98}{s}; (iii) S2 mean daily invalidation volume (\(2685\) median tiles, \SI{126.73}{MB}): \SIrange{5.13}{10.69}{s}; and (iv) S3 daily SQL patch transmission (gzip estimate, \SI{5.07}{MB}): \SIrange{0.21}{0.43}{s}. These results indicate that, in practical mobile operation, compact SQL patch delivery is typically faster than broad tile-bundle refresh for equivalent daily update coverage.

The daily benchmark is designed to be extended into a weekly longitudinal protocol with regular baseline checkpoints and intervening incremental updates. This will quantify whether reduced update effort is preserved over longer horizons while retaining low-latency query behavior.

\section{Conclusion}
Under a 50\%/50\% decision rule (equal weight on query latency and update effort), this study selects S3 as the preferred operating point. Query performance uses on-device mean latency, while update effort uses scenario-specific incremental-update proxies from Section 5 (partition invalidation for S1/S2, simulated patch runtime for S3/S4), normalized to a common \([0,1]\) scale before weighting. The resulting ranking is \(S3 \prec S4 \prec S2 \ll S1\) (lower is better).

The next development cycle targets corridor-HMM emissions rather than another topology expansion. Priority 1 is to keep the shared-node corridor representation and publish the same \texttt{way\_links}/\texttt{corridor\_pairs} topology in every S3 region. Priority 2 is to add stronger tunnel-commit evidence inside already selected corridor chains, especially delayed signal-quality deterioration and along-chain progress after portal entry. Priority 3 is to evaluate whether a learned corridor-transition gate adds measurable benefit over the selected shared-node corridor HMM and the simpler connected baseline on the expanded field-log corpus.
